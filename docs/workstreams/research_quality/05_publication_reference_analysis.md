# Publication-quality research references and comparative analysis

**Purpose:** establish a concrete reference set for E6 visual argument and publication-quality reporting. This supersedes the earlier assumption that visual quality could be represented mainly by clean slide templates.

## Reference collection

### Pew Research Center — favourite data visualisations of 2025

URL: https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/12/15/our-favorite-data-visualizations-of-2025/

Why it matters:

- Pew explicitly optimises graphics for clear takeaways, concise communication, reader engagement and research storytelling.
- Chart choice follows the analytical relationship rather than a standard dashboard component library.
- Standard charts are enriched with contextual dots, lines and annotations when the extra comparison changes interpretation.
- Complex forms such as alluvials, bullet charts, rose plots and beeswarms are used selectively when they explain a relationship better than a conventional bar/line chart.
- Annotation is part of the evidence, not decorative commentary around the evidence.

Velocity lesson: simplicity is not the goal by itself. The visual should make the research comparison cognitively obvious.

### YouGov — Best Brand Rankings 2026

URL: https://yougov.com/en-gb/reports/best-brand-rankings

Why it matters:

- Directly relevant brand-tracking research built on long-term BrandIndex measurement across 28+ markets and millions of survey responses.
- Strong page-level hierarchy and branded editorial pacing.
- Separates ranking/level from change/momentum.
- Combines multiple brand-health dimensions: Impression, Quality, Value, Satisfaction, Recommend and Reputation.
- The 2026 product explicitly adds qualitative consumer explanation to answer *why* a brand performs as it does.

Velocity lesson: a brand report should not stop at a ranked KPI. It should connect competitive position, movement, drivers and interpretation.

### Deloitte — Digital Consumer Trends 2026

URL: https://www.deloitte.com/uk/en/Industries/tmt/research/digital-consumer-trends.html

Why it matters:

- Long-running UK consumer research (17 years in 2026), organised around substantive themes rather than questionnaire sections.
- Headlines make interpretive propositions: e.g. device transformation may be complete because no new form factors are breaking through.
- Uses individual survey measures to support a broader argument rather than narrating each measure separately.
- Provides methodology and weighting context without letting methodology dominate the editorial flow.

Velocity lesson: commentary should add synthesis. If prose only repeats a plotted percentage, it has failed.

### Deloitte — Consumer Tracker Q1 2026

URL: https://www.deloitte.com/uk/en/Industries/consumer/research/consumer-tracker.html

Why it matters:

- Recurring tracker is framed around the current consumer story: confidence deterioration and discretionary-spending pressure.
- Latest-wave movement is interpreted against multi-year history and economic context.
- Demographic detail is available for exploration but does not replace the primary editorial story.

Velocity lesson: trackers need context and longitudinal memory, not a latest-wave dump.

### Ipsos — What Worries the World

URL: https://www.ipsos.com/en-nl/what-worries-world-december-2025

Why it matters:

- Monthly tracker across ~30 countries with more than a decade of history.
- Reporting combines latest level, rank, movement, country variation and historical extrema.
- Key findings are selected before detailed country/data views.

Velocity lesson: recurring research gains value when current observations are located within an interpretable historical frame.

## Comparative diagnosis of the rejected SBT-001 decks

### Failure 1 — template cleanliness substituted for hierarchy

The first reference deck was cleaner than generic automated research but still treated slides as arrangements of components. The second attempt removed containers and became flatter rather than more editorial.

Publication-quality hierarchy should be:

1. page-level proposition;
2. dominant evidence;
3. contextual/counter evidence;
4. synthesis or implication;
5. local methodological qualification.

Not every element deserves equal visual weight.

### Failure 2 — chart type followed data shape rather than research question

Five brands over five waves does not automatically imply five line charts. The question might instead be:

- who has momentum relative to current position? → position × change map;
- who changed between two strategic moments? → slopegraph/dumbbell;
- whether an event created an inflection? → annotated time series;
- whether two universes diverge? → paired/juxtaposed measures;
- whether a funnel converts? → staged/funnel comparison.

The analytical relationship must be selected before the chart family.

### Failure 3 — findings were mistaken for a narrative

`Pulse up`, `Northstar CX down`, `Harbour youth down` are valid findings, not yet a complete research story.

A publication-quality chapter should generally answer:

- What changed?
- Where/for whom?
- What evidence triangulates or challenges the interpretation?
- Why does it matter to this brief?
- What remains uncertain?

### Failure 4 — commentary repeated evidence

Bad commentary: `Pulse consideration increased.`

Useful commentary: `Pulse is becoming mentally available to younger consumers faster than it is converting them into customers.`

The second statement synthesises multiple measures. Commentary earns space only when it adds interpretation, comparison, mechanism hypothesis, counterevidence or implication.

### Failure 5 — narrative rhythm was uniform

Automated decks often repeat `headline + chart + commentary` for every page. Strong publications vary page function:

- proposition / opener;
- competitive landscape;
- hero trend;
- segment explanation;
- evidence synthesis;
- contrast/divergence;
- longitudinal diagnosis;
- implications.

Consistency should come from typography, annotation and evidence conventions, not identical composition.

## Publication-quality design principles for Velocity

| Capability | Mechanical output | Publication-quality target |
|---|---|---|
| Hierarchy | every result gets a component | one dominant idea per page |
| Chart selection | chart matches datatype | visual form matches analytical relationship |
| Narrative | questionnaire order | argument order |
| Commentary | repeats values | synthesises evidence |
| Context | latest wave | baseline, benchmark, segment or history |
| Density | uniform | varies deliberately by page function |
| Colour | brand palette everywhere | emphasis encodes meaning |
| Restraint | reports what is available | edits aggressively and silently |

## Important restraint principle

Trustworthiness should usually be **demonstrated through editorial behaviour, not self-described**.

If a low-base, inflated or unrepresentative result does not deserve the client's attention, the normal publication-quality act is to omit it. The report should not congratulate itself for doing so. Methodological notes belong where they materially change interpretation, not as a slide explaining the model's virtue.

This principle supersedes the proposed SBT-001 `what we deliberately did not conclude` slide. That slide is removed from the redesigned storyboard.

## Consequence for E6

E6 should not ask only whether a deck is attractive or free of layout defects. It should evaluate whether each page embodies a strong **visual argument**:

`communication goal → analytical relationship → visual form → evidence hierarchy → annotation strategy → commentary role → local qualification`.
